How to use this guide

You do not need to be a professional physicist. If you have met sines, basic algebra, and the idea that light can behave as a wave, you are in the right place. We build intuition with moving pictures first, then connect each picture to the equations.

Central idea: for a quantum particle such as an electron, we do not predict a single definite position. We predict how likely it is to be found at each place. That information is encoded in a wave function ψ (psi) — and from ψ we obtain probabilities using Born’s rule (see section 9).

1. Classical waves first

Before electrons, get comfortable with a plain travelling wave. Along a line, height y at position x and time t can be written:

Travelling wave y(x, t) = A sin(kx − ωt + φ)

Read it as y = A sin(Θ) with Θ = kx − ωt + φ: Θ is the angle; A sin(Θ) is the height on the y-axis at that x and t.

  • A — amplitude (how tall the wave is)
  • k (wave number) — how rapidly the pattern repeats in space; related to λ (lambda, wavelength) by λ = 2π/k
  • ω (omega, angular frequency) — how fast the phase angle changes in time (radians per second); ordinary frequency f (hertz) is f = ω/(2π) — see below
  • v — wave speed along the line: v = ω/k (also v = fλ) — below
  • φ (phi, phase) — see below; how the wave is lined up in space and time

The equation in plain language

The whole formula is one familiar pattern: height on the y-axis = amplitude × sine of an angle. Write the angle as Θ (theta):

Θ = kx − ωt + φ ⇒ y(x, t) = A sin(Θ)

Pick a place x and a moment t. Work out Θ from k, ω, and φ. sin(Θ) is a number between −1 and 1 (where you are on the repeating cycle). Multiply by A to get the actual height y on the vertical axis. The symbols kx, −ωt, and φ only exist to build that angle; they are not separate “height” terms.

Each piece of Θ = kx − ωt + φ is one idea:

  1. ① A — amplitude
    How tall the wave is. A = 0.75 means crests reach 0.75 above the middle line.
  2. ② sin(Θ) — the repeating shape
    Θ = kx − ωt + φ is the phase angle at that x and t. The sine curve repeats every 2π radians in Θ; y = A sin(Θ) turns that angle into height on y (see the unit circle demo).
  3. ③ kx — ripples along the line
    As x increases, kx grows and the pattern repeats in space. Large k → short wavelength λ = 2π/k.
  4. ④ −ωt — the pattern moves
    As t increases, −ωt shifts the shape along +x (wave travelling to the right).
  5. ⑤ φ — phase (offset)
    A constant added inside the sine. It slides the whole pattern along x, or at one point sets where you are on the cycle.

So at any (x, t): compute Θ = kx − ωt + φ, then y = A sin(Θ). The three parts of Θ are kx (position in space), −ωt (motion in time), and φ (offset) — only the angle goes into sine; A scales the result to a height.

Unit circle — how y follows Θ

On a circle of radius 1, pick an angle Θ from the positive x-axis. The point on the circle has coordinates (cos Θ, sin Θ). The vertical coordinate sin Θ is exactly the factor that goes into the wave height; multiply by amplitude A to get y = A sin(Θ). Frequency f and wave speed v

The same angle Θ from the unit circle appears inside the travelling wave. Fix a place on the rope (x constant). Then Θ = −ωt + (constants), so as time passes the angle spins the same way as on the circle. One full turn of the circle (Θ increasing by 2π radians) is one complete oscillation up and down at that point.

Physicists often write ω in radians per second (how fast the angle changes). In everyday units we count cycles per second — that is the frequency f in hertz (Hz):

Ordinary frequency f = ω / (2π) ⇔ ω = 2πf

A cycle is one full 2π swing of the angle, so ω radians per second means ω/(2π) cycles per second. The time for one cycle is the period T = 1/f = 2π/ω.

In space, the pattern repeats every wavelength λ = 2π/k. In time, it repeats every period T. A crest (or any fixed point on the shape) travels along the line at the wave speed:

Wave speed v = ω / k ⇔ v = f λ

The second form is intuitive: each second the wave completes f cycles, and each cycle is λ long in space, so the pattern advances fλ metres per second. You can check: ω/k = (2πf)/(2π/λ) = fλ.

  1. ① At fixed x — link to the unit circle
    Height is y = A sin(Θ) with Θ changing like the angle on the unit circle. When ω = 2π rad/s, the angle goes around once per second, so f = 1 Hz.
  2. ② Crests move — where v = ω/k comes from
    A crest stays at the top of the sine, so kx − ωt + φ = π/2 (plus whole multiples of 2π). Solve for x: x = (π/2 − φ + ωt)/k. As t increases by 1 s, x increases by ω/k — that is v.
  3. ③ Same numbers, two views
    k and ω are radians per metre and per second; λ and f are “per cycle” in space and time. Use whichever pair feels clearer — they are tied by 2π.

What is φ (phase)?

At a fixed position — take x = 0 — the height is

y(0, t) = A sin(−ωt + φ)

At t = 0 that is y(0, 0) = A sin(φ). So φ tells you where on the cycle the point starts on the vertical axis:

  • φ = 0 → sin(0) = 0 (sine-like: zero height, rising) — like standard sin
  • φ = π/2 → sin(π/2) = 1 (at the top) — like cos at the origin
  • φ = π → zero again but falling; φ = 3π/2 → at the bottom

More generally, φ is an offset in the cycle. Two waves with the same A, k, and ω but different φ are the same shape shifted sideways — or, at one place on the rope, offset on the y-axis by a fraction of a cycle. That offset is what matters for interference.

    x = 0, t = 0, φ = 0: y = sin(0) = 0 (on the midline, going up).
    Same place with φ = π/2: y = sin(π/2) = 1 (crest at the origin).
  • Example B — a crest elsewhere, frozen in time
    t = 0, φ = 0. A crest needs kx = π/2, so x = (π/2)/k ≈ 0.98 → y = 1.
  • Example C — why it “travels”

2. Wave–particle duality

Light and matter both show wave-like and particle-like behaviour — but not as a simple “sometimes a ball, sometimes a ripple” in the same measurement.

  • Particle-like: light arrives in discrete packets (photons); electrons hit a detector one at a time as dots.
  • Wave-like: light and electrons produce interference and diffraction — patterns that require adding wave amplitudes.

The punchline: a single electron is not a tiny ball that secretly goes through only one slit. Its quantum description can include contributions associated with many paths; those contributions combine (interfere) before we turn them into a probability of where the dot appears.

3. Water waves: one slit, then two

Nothing teaches interference like ripples on water. A vibrating source sends circular waves. A barrier with a slit acts as a new source (Huygens’ principle: every point on a wavefront can spawn outgoing ripples). With one slit you see a spreading fan — diffraction. With two slits, ripples from each slit overlap: where crests align the water is higher (a bright fringe). Where crest meets trough the displacements cancel — the waves add to almost zero. Those dark fringes are therefore very slim lines (complete cancellation), not wide bands like the bright regions.

5. Feynman: sum over paths

Richard Feynman offered a vivid way to think about the double slit: a photon or electron does not travel a single classical path. In the quantum calculation we (conceptually) allow every possible path from source to screen — straight, wiggly, via either slit, and everything in between. Each path carries a little probability amplitude (a complex number with a phase). The observed probability is built from the sum of all those contributions.

Paths that differ slightly in length pick up slightly different phases. Where many paths add in phase, detection is likely; where they swirl out of phase, they cancel. That reproduces the same fringes as wave interference — without insisting the particle “went both ways” as a classical object.

Animated — sample of possible paths; colour = phase

Many paths contribute; no single path is “the” trajectory before detection.

Path-integral idea (schematic) amplitude to arrive ≈ Σ (over paths) eiS/ℏ

S is the classical action along a path; ℏ (h-bar) sets the scale on which phase differences matter. You do not need to evaluate the sum for this guide — the picture is what matters.

6. Matter waves (de Broglie)

If a particle has momentum p, Louis de Broglie associated a wavelength:

de Broglie λ = h / p

Here h (Planck constant) is the same constant that links photon energy to frequency. Because h is tiny, everyday objects have wavelengths too short to show interference. Slow electrons have λ comparable to atomic scales — that is when wave effects dominate.

Units matter. Use p in kg·m/s before substituting into λ = h/p.

7. Bridge: from classical travelling wave to ψ

In section 1 the rope height was a real sine wave y = A sin(kx − ωt + φ). In section 6, de Broglie gave a wavelength for matter. Here we connect those ideas to the quantum wave function ψ — step by step, without skipping the jump.

  1. ① Same phase angle Θ as before
    Define Θ = kx − ωt + φ exactly as in the classical wave. Space (through k) and time (through ω) enter in the same way.
  2. ② Hide the sine inside a complex exponential
    Euler’s formula: eiΘ = cos(Θ) + i sin(Θ). So the classical height is the Imaginary part of a complex wave: y = A sin(Θ) = Imaginary part of A eiΘ. (You could equally use the Real part and get a cosine — same physics, different initial phase.)
  3. ③ Name the full complex amplitude ψ
    Define ψ = A eiΘ = A ei(kx − ωt + φ). Then Real part = A cos(Θ), Imaginary part = A sin(Θ). The classical rope height you see is one slice of this complex object — usually the Imaginary part.
  4. ④ Matter waves supply k and ω
    For a particle, de Broglie sets λ = h/p, hence k = 2π/λ. Quantum mechanics also links energy to frequency (E = hf, and ω = 2πf). So the shape of the travelling factor is the same; the constants are fixed by particle momentum and energy.
  5. ⑤ What changes in quantum mechanics
    ψ is still a complex travelling wave with the same k and ω idea — but it is not the height of something you shake. Detectors care about |ψ|² (probability density), not the Real or Imaginary part alone. Localized particles use a packet (envelope × wave), as in the animations below and in section 8.

Same Θ — classical sine matches Imaginary part of ψ = A eiΘ

Classical height y = A sin(Θ) Real part of A eiΘ (cosine) Imaginary part (sine) — same as y ψ = A eiΘ (complex amplitude)
Plain summary. Classical waves gave you sin(Θ). Complex numbers bundle sin and cos together as eiΘ. Quantum mechanics calls that bundle ψ and uses |ψ|² for probabilities — but the travelling phase kx − ωt is the same idea you already learned.

8. The wave function ψ

Now that ψ is connected to the classical wave (section 7), we spell out what “complex” means and how interference works. For one particle in one dimension, the state is ψ(x, t) — generally complex at each point. Think of it as the recipe for how contributions combine before a measurement, not as something you read off a meter directly.

What does “generally complex” mean?

At each position x and time t, ψ is a complex number. You can write it in two equivalent ways:

  • Rectangular form: ψ = a + i b, where a is the Real part and b is the Imaginary part (with i the square root of −1).
  • Polar form: ψ = |ψ| eiφ — a magnitude |ψ| and a phase φ (how far around the cycle you are).

Real and Imaginary are not two different waves. They are two coordinates of the same arrow in the complex plane — like east and north components of one vector on a map.

Complex plane — one arrow for ψ at a chosen position x

ψ (one complex number) Real part Imaginary part |ψ| length → |ψ|² for probability

Why is ψ complex?

Interference — the heart of the double-slit story — depends on phase: whether two contributions arrive crest-on-crest or crest-on-trough. A single ordinary (real) number at each point is not enough:

  • A real amplitude can only represent “in phase” or “exactly out of phase” (positive vs negative) relative to another real wave.
  • Quantum mechanics needs the full range of relative phases (anywhere from 0 to 2π), because path length, energy, and time all shift the phase continuously.
  • Using a complex ψ is the compact way to store both “how big” and “where on the cycle” at once. The Schrödinger equation naturally produces complex solutions.

So “generally complex” simply means: in almost every real situation, ψ has a non-zero Imaginary part as well as a Real part. Classical water height is a real wave; the quantum wave function is one step richer.

Real, Imaginary, and what you measure

The animation shows a snapshot of one wave packet. The Real and Imaginary curves wiggle along x and oscillate in time. The quantity that feeds Born’s rule is the squared magnitude, which uses both parts together:

Squared magnitude |ψ|² = (Real part)² + (Imaginary part)²

Detectors do not print “Real” or “Imaginary” separately. They respond to |ψ|² (probability density) — see section 9.

Snapshot of ψ(x) — Real part, Imaginary part, and |ψ|²

Real part of ψ Imaginary part of ψ |ψ|² (probability density)

Where does interference come from? (Not Real vs Imaginary)

This is the crucial distinction. Interference does not mean the Real part fighting the Imaginary part of a single ψ. Those two parts belong to one amplitude; they combine when you form |ψ|².

Interference appears when two (or more) separate contributions add — for example waves from slit 1 and slit 2:

Total amplitude at a point on the screen ψ = ψ₁ + ψ₂

Each of ψ₁ and ψ₂ is complex (each has its own Real and Imaginary parts). They can arrive with different phases. You add the full complex amplitudes first, then square:

probability density = |ψ₁ + ψ₂|²

That is different from adding probabilities separately, |ψ₁|² + |ψ₂|². The extra piece — the interference term — involves the phases of ψ₁ and ψ₂ together. It is worked out step by step in section 9; the ripple tank and detection-screen demos show the same idea for water height and for quantum dots.

9. Born’s rule and probability density

Probability vs probability density

These words sound alike but mean different things:

  • Probability is a number between 0 and 1 for a whole event (“found between here and here”).
  • Probability density |ψ(x)|² tells you how densely probability is spread along the axis — like mass density tells you how mass is spread in space.

To obtain a probability for a small interval, multiply density by width:

Probability in a tiny strip P(x in [x₀, x₀ + Δx]) ≈ |ψ(x₀)|² · Δx   (when Δx is small)

For the whole line, the total must be 100%:

Normalisation ∫ |ψ(x)|² dx = 1

The integral adds up infinitely many thin strips — exactly the idea shown in the animation below.

Drag the window — see |ψ|² × Δx as a probability sliver

Born’s rule

Max Born proposed: the probability density for position is the squared magnitude of the wave function:

Born rule (1D) ρ(x) = |ψ(x)|²

Why we add amplitudes before squaring

Suppose two slits contribute ψ₁ and ψ₂ at some point on the screen. The total amplitude is ψ = ψ₁ + ψ₂. The probability density uses the combined amplitude:

  • Step 1 — add amplitudes
    ψ = ψ₁ + ψ₂
  • Step 2 — square magnitude
    |ψ|² = |ψ₁ + ψ₂|²
  • Step 3 — expand (Real and Imaginary parts of both ψ₁ and ψ₂)
    |ψ₁ + ψ₂|² = |ψ₁|² + |ψ₂|² + 2 × (real part of ψ₁*ψ₂)

The last term is the interference term: it depends on the relative phase between the two complex amplitudes (how their Real and Imaginary parts line up). It can be positive (bright fringe) or negative (dark fringe — near-complete cancellation, hence a slim band). Without it, you would only get two overlapping blobs — never the full pattern. This is not “Real interfering with Imaginary” within a single ψ — see section 8.

10. Particle in a box

A particle between walls at x = 0 and x = L has standing-wave solutions with integer n = 1, 2, 3, …:

Energy level n ψₙ(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx / L)

Then |ψₙ|² is the probability density. For n = 1 the electron is most likely near the centre; for n = 2 there are two favoured regions and zero probability at the centre (a node).

11. Schrödinger equation (gentle)

ψ is found by solving the Schrödinger equation. In one dimension:

Time-dependent iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ

Here ℏ and Ĥ (H-hat, Hamiltonian) encode how kinetic and potential energy shape the evolution. For a definite energy E, write ψ(x,t) = ψ(x) e^{-iEt/ℏ} to obtain:

Time-independent Ĥψ = Eψ

The particle-in-a-box example is a simple solution of Ĥψ = Eψ. Hydrogen is richer — but the workflow is identical: solve for ψ, then read probabilities from |ψ|².
